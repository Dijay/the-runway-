I
Part one
What changes when the engineering transforms

Every discipline a cloud team spent a decade perfecting still exists in the agentic world, but the unit of truth changes, and so does every artifact the team hands over. Before we can decide what to ship, we have to see what testing became.

01The comfortable lie

The leaderboard lies.

Rank anything by a single number and you crown a winner. It feels rigorous, it's a score, after all, but a single axis quietly rewards the most expensive, most over engineered system in the room, because the cheapest way to buy one more point of accuracy is almost always to spend more compute. The leaderboard never shows you that bill.

You already know this animal in two habitats. In the wild it's Chatbot Arena, MMLU, SWE-bench, the vendor crowing "#1 on the benchmark." In the enterprise it wears a suit.

In the world
  • Public Elo boards ranking models head to head
  • Static benchmarks, one accuracy number, one ranking
  • "State of the art", measured on accuracy alone
In your bank
  • The POC bake off won by the top sample set score
  • The RFP scorecard that crowns a vendor
  • The demo that dazzled the steering committee

Any time a single number crowns a winner, that's a leaderboard, and most banks run them without the name.

02Why the old scoreboard broke

Intelligence answers differently every time.

Software was deterministic: one input, one output, one verdict. You wrote a test, it passed or failed, and green stayed green until someone changed the code. Intelligence doesn't work that way. Ask the same question twice and you get two answers, both plausible, rarely identical.

So the unit of truth changes underneath you. "Correct" stops being a checkmark and becomes a distribution, how often, how close, how reliably. And once quality is a distribution measured against several things you care about at once, no single axis can rank it honestly. The scoreboard didn't get harder to read. It stopped being the right shape.

The mapping gets a cloud leader to "I understand this." Four differences get them to "I need to fund this differently", and they're where the new value lives.

Delta 01 · Verdict → distribution

Tests don't pass. They score.

You manage pass rate distributions and confidence thresholds, not green checkmarks. Closer to statistical process control than xUnit, a new skill on an old instinct.

Delta 02 · The oracle problem

The tester needs testing.

"Correct" is often judgment based, so an LLM judge scores the output, and must itself be calibrated against human experts. A quality layer classic QE never needed.

Delta 03 · The ground moves

Green today proves nothing about next month.

Model updates, data drift, and context shifts change behaviour even when you ship nothing. Evaluation stops being a phase and becomes permanent infrastructure.

Delta 04 · Fixtures → IP

The test assets are the moat.

Golden datasets encode the client's domain judgment, the most durable, defensible artifact in the engagement. Old fixtures were throwaway; these are a balance sheet asset.

II
Part two
How you decide what to ship

Knowing the disciplines transformed is not the same as knowing which agent to deploy. For that you need a different shape than a leaderboard, one that scores quality and cost together, keeps itself honest, and admits what it can't decide for you.

04A better shape

Plot quality against cost, and the truth inverts.

Give every agent two axes instead of one, how well it does the task, and what it costs to get there, and the impressive systems stop impressing. This is Pareto dominance, borrowed from economics: one configuration dominates another when it's at least as good on every axis and strictly better on one. The set of configurations nothing else beats is the frontier. Everything below it is simply waste, paying more, or waiting longer, for quality you could have had for less.

Two landmark results from 2024 drew this line for the field. Princeton's AI Agents That Matter rescored leading agent architectures on accuracy and cost and found that simple baselines Pareto dominated the elaborate ones, the complex systems bought almost no accuracy for a great deal more money. Their most quoted number: agents of similar accuracy differed in cost by nearly a hundredfold, yet cost was rarely reported at all.

Berkeley's RouteLLM proved the deployment mirror. By routing each query to the cheapest model that could answer it well, it held roughly 95% of frontier quality at over 85% lower cost, the frontier itself pushed outward. One paper says measure on the frontier; the other says deploy on it.

A leaderboard ranks agents. A frontier shows you the tradeoff, and which one you'd actually ship.

But a frontier is a map, not a route.

The chart tells you where the optimal point sits. It doesn't tell you where your task lands on it, and for real work, you can't know in advance. "Fix xyz bug" might be a one line edge case, or a multi file race condition investigation that forces a rearchitecture. The opening prompt doesn't carry that information; only investigating the code reveals it. So the operating point can't be chosen once, up front. It has to be found in flight.

That turns routing into a live control loop, not a deployment setting. The pattern has a shape: cascade and escalate. Default every task down to the cheapest model that can make progress, exploring, reading, routine edits, and escalate back up to a frontier model the moment difficulty spikes: real ambiguity, a cross cutting change, or a context compaction moment where switching is cheap anyway. The frontier model keeps ownership of planning and final review; the cheaper model does the legwork and hands the hard parts back.

This is already shipping, and the debate around it is live. The Runway takes up the routing argument directly, Scott Wu's case that you can't know a task's difficulty from the request, Josh Hedtke's harder claim that grading a task before starting is just guessing, and why what they retire is prediction, not routing. Here we stay on the evals question: what makes escalation trustworthy once you've stopped predicting.

The evidence points one way. Cognition's Devin Fusion runs a cheaper "sidekick" alongside a frontier agent and reroutes dynamically mid session, shifting back toward the smarter model when a task turns out harder than it looked; Cognition reports this trims cost 30 to 40% while holding frontier quality. Three figures are worth keeping straight, because they measure different things: intelligent routing in general is estimated to cut frontier spend 30 to 70% (the range cited in the hub brief); cascade and escalate specifically lands around 30 to 40% (Devin's self reported number); and RouteLLM, the peer reviewed upper bound, held ~95% of quality at over 85% lower cost on its benchmark. The direction is unanimous even where the magnitudes differ: the control plane's job isn't to pick a model, it's to move between them intelligently, per task, as the task reveals itself.

For the bank

The deeper implication isn't technical, it's commercial. If a task's difficulty is unknowable until you've investigated it, its cost is unknowable too, which means a fixed scope SOW quotes a price against a difficulty nobody has measured. The disciplined answer is investigate then commit: a scoped discovery gate that sizes the work before anyone commits to building it. Cascade and escalate is that principle expressed in silicon. The crew change and week four gate engagement shapes are the same principle expressed in a contract.

And there is a risk edge the hub brief names: a router can be right 99% of the time and still lose money if the 1% it gets wrong holds the high stakes cases. Bounding that tail is an evals problem. Escalation is only as safe as your ability to score the cases where it matters, which is why, for a bank, evals aren't the sidecar to routing. They're the thing that makes routing something you're allowed to run.

05How it stays honest

A frontier you don't guard is just a slower leaderboard.

The frontier is powerful, and naïvely applied it's dangerous. Two truths keep it honest, and a POV that names them reads as senior rather than salesy.

Some axes you trade. Some you never trade.

The frontier framing assumes every dimension is negotiable, a little accuracy for a lot less cost. In a regulated bank, that breaks. "Doesn't leak PII," "doesn't give unlicensed advice," "doesn't invent a number on a client statement" aren't points on the frontier. They're floors.

So the honest model has two layers: hard gates every candidate must clear to get onto the chart at all, then the frontier, where the real tradeoffs get optimized among the survivors. Treating a safety requirement as just another axis is how an AI program becomes a regulatory incident.

Evals draw the frontier, but first they decide who's allowed on it
The frontier has to be guarded, or it stops being true.

Every measured target carries a catch with a name: Goodhart's Law, when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Point a team at a golden dataset and they'll climb it: tuning to your cases, learning the judge's tells, optimizing for the test instead of the task. The score rises; real quality doesn't.

The defense isn't to abandon the frontier, it's to treat the golden dataset like a security asset: held out, rotated, versioned, never the thing you train against. A frontier is only honest as long as the agent has never seen the questions.

A frontier you optimize against is just a slower leaderboard

And one more motion, easy to miss: the frontier moves. Models update beneath you, data drifts, cheaper models close the gap, today's optimal operating point is next quarter's dominated one. That's not a flaw in the method; it's the reason the control plane exists. It lets you ride the frontier down instead of replatforming every time the market shifts.

06The part nobody admits

It's human judgment all the way down.

Here's the admission that earns the rest of the argument. The frontier looks objective, the dominance math is neutral, a point is non dominated or it isn't. But every input to that math is a value call. Which axes you plot is a statement of what you value. The golden labels are someone's opinion of "correct." The judge's rubric is taste, codified. Even the threshold is a risk appetite decision wearing a number's clothing.

The clearest proof lives in coding agents. Two models can both pass the same test and still not be interchangeable, one writes idiomatic, maintainable code in your house style; the other writes something correct but alien that the next engineer has to unpick. A pass/fail eval calls them equal. A senior reviewer never would. Which is why practitioners building production coding agents now argue evals must score style and behaviour, not just correctness, and "style" is simply encoded taste. The rubric is where a team writes down what it means by "good," and no benchmark hands you that for free.

So Pareto doesn't remove the subjectivity. It relocates it, out of a single smug score and up into explicit, auditable choices about what you measure and what you'll tolerate. It's Hume's old fact and value split made operational: the frontier hands you the facts; you still supply the ought. Which is the honest version of the whole discipline, we didn't build the eval apparatus to end the argument. We built it to have a better one.

The frontier doesn't make the choice objective. It makes the subjectivity honest.

07Where this lives

Evals are how you draw it.

Dominance is a claim on two axes at once, which is exactly why it can't be asserted from a demo. The gateway meters the cost. The model registry catalogues the candidates. But only an eval harness measures the quality, repeatedly, against a guarded dataset, at a defined bar. Strip the evals out and the frontier collapses back into anecdote: which agent felt better in the room.

That's the through line of The Runway. The control plane isn't a diagram of boxes; it's the machinery that turns "we think this is good" into a decision an executive can sign and a regulator can audit. Evals are the instrument that makes the whole architecture decidable.
